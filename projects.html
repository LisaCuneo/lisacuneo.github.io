PROJECTS

Mathematical approaches applied to biological measurements

I am interested in the mathematical layer between what an experiment can physically measure and what we want to learn about a biological system. Depending on the information available in the data, this may lead to a model-based inverse problem, an optimization method, physics-aware learning, or the design and control of the measurement itself.

MODEL-BASED & OPTIMIZATION

Exploiting a known physical forward model.

Here, the relationship between measurement and unknown is represented through an explicit physical or statistical model. Losses, regularization, constraints and numerical optimization then determine how the latent quantity is recovered.

01.1

REGULARIZATION BY NOISE

Can multiple noise realizations reveal information about the sample?

Time-resolved SPAD detection preserves the arrival time of individual photons. Partitioning the photon stream into disjoint temporal windows turns a single acquisition into multiple independent Poisson realizations of the same underlying object.

In Image Scanning Microscopy, the SPAD array produces a 5×5 set of detector images, each providing a blurred observation of the same specimen. These measurements must then be combined and deconvolved to recover a higher-resolution estimate of the underlying object rather than the optically blurred image formed by the microscope.

Richardson–Lucy (RL) is a natural reconstruction method for photon-counting data because it follows the physical forward model and Poisson statistics. Its iterations are, however, semi-convergent: after an initial improvement, prolonged optimization progressively fits the measurement noise. In practice, RL therefore requires either early stopping or an explicit prior on the unknown image.

Regularization by Noise replaces morphology-based priors with agreement across independent realizations: reproducible features are preserved, while realization-specific fluctuations are suppressed. The prior is therefore statistical rather than morphological, allowing the same regularization principle to be applied across very different biological structures. The resulting coupled variational problem also leads to a nontrivial optimization task, for which we are developing a dedicated algorithm with convergence guarantees.

Poisson inverse problems · morphology-agnostic regularization · variational modelling · numerical optimization · convergence analysis

01.2 · ONGOING

PSF DISTILLER & BLIND DECONVOLUTION

Can the microscope response be recovered directly from the data?

The point-spread function (PSF) describes the optical response of the microscope. Aberrations distort its shape, so recovering the PSF also reveals how the imaging system departs from the ideal model.

CALIBRATION FROM A BEAD

A fluorescent bead provides a known calibration object. In ISM, the 5×5 detector array produces 25 related PSFs whose shifts depend on detector position but whose optical content comes from the same excitation and emission paths. I fit the complete PSF grid with a physics-based model and express the recovered wavefront distortions through Zernike coefficients. On simulated data, the estimated excitation and emission coefficients can be compared directly with the ground truth.

BLIND DECONVOLUTION

With a biological sample, the object is no longer known: the 25 detector images are blurred by PSFs that are themselves unknown. I therefore treat reconstruction as a blind multi-image inverse problem, jointly estimating the specimen and the microscope response from the same ISM acquisition while exploiting the shared image-formation physics across detector channels.

Point-spread function · optical aberrations · Zernike polynomials · microscope calibration · blind deconvolution

01.3

LIFETIME UNMIXING · MODEL-BASED OPTIMIZATION

Can reconstruction and lifetime unmixing be solved as a single inverse problem?

Time-resolved microscopy acquires an x–y–t dataset: TCSPC — Time-Correlated Single-Photon Counting — adds photon-arrival time to the spatial measurement. When fluorophores overlap spectrally, differences in their fluorescence decay provide an additional contrast mechanism for multi-label separation.

APPROACH 1 · JOINT OPTIMIZATION

In time-resolved Image Scanning Microscopy, I formulate spatial reconstruction and temporal unmixing as one spatio-temporal inverse problem, rather than reconstructing space and analysing time in separate steps.

Reference decay signatures are used to separate the mixed signal while the detector-array information recovers spatial detail. The output is therefore one separated, super-resolved reconstruction for each molecular target rather than a pixel-wise lifetime map.

Spatio-temporal inverse problems · TCSPC · fluorescence lifetime · multi-target imaging · super-resolution

PHYSICS-AWARE LEARNING

Embedding measurement physics into learning.

Data-driven models learn complex relationships directly from microscopy measurements, while physical knowledge of image formation and signal structure remains part of the model design. Depending on the problem, this information enters through the architecture, the representation or the reconstruction constraints, keeping the learned solution connected to the acquisition.

02.1

LIFETIME UNMIXING · EXPLAINABLE-BY-DESIGN LEARNING

Can the same unmixing problem be learned without hiding the physics?

A second route is to learn the separation directly from time-resolved data. The network receives the complete W × H × T sequence and processes spatial and temporal information together, rather than fitting each pixel independently.

The architecture is explainable by design: separate branches predict a spatial component Ai and a fluorescence-decay parameter τi for each species. A deterministic part then rebuilds the corresponding time-resolved components and sums them back to the measured sequence, keeping the physical meaning of the intermediate outputs explicit.

Because training is constrained through reconstruction of the input sequence, the method does not require a ground-truth decomposition for every measurement. The original two-component formulation was subsequently extended toward the separation of up to four spectrally overlapping fluorophores.

Fluorescence lifetime · explainable-by-design · physics-aware learning · spectral unmixing · spatio-temporal imaging

02.2

SINGLE-MOLECULE LOCALIZATION MICROSCOPY · BACKGROUND REMOVAL

Can background be removed before localization without erasing the molecular signal?

Single-Molecule Localization Microscopy (SMLM) reconstructs a super-resolved image from sparse emitters detected across many frames. Background is therefore not only a visual nuisance: it can perturb emitter detection and localization and propagate into false, missed or merged localizations in the final reconstruction.

I explored two complementary representations matched to different background regimes. Scattering networks use a fixed multiscale wavelet transform to encode spatial structure before a learned decoder reconstructs the molecule-dominated image. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), instead, uses the full temporal stack to separate coherent, slowly varying background from the faster molecular signal.

The two approaches become useful in different situations: SVD is better suited to temporally stable backgrounds, whereas the scattering-based method is more effective for spatially complex or blinking background. The purpose of this preprocessing is to prevent background structures from being interpreted as real emitters. Without an effective separation, background can generate spurious localizations, hide true molecules or merge nearby events, ultimately distorting the reconstructed biological structure. This is especially clear in the synthetic grid experiment, where false detections caused by the background disrupt an otherwise regular molecular pattern.

EARLIER WORK

Different physics, the same concern with what can be inferred.

Before focusing on computational microscopy, I worked on inverse problems arising from electromagnetic measurements of brain activity. The physical system was different, but the relationship between measurement design and inference was already central.

03.1

MASTER THESIS · ON-SCALP MEG · SENSOR-ARRAY DESIGN

How many sensors are needed, and where should they be placed, to localize brain activity accurately?

Magnetoencephalography measures the magnetic fields generated by neural currents outside the head. Optically pumped magnetometers can operate much closer to the scalp than conventional cryogenic sensors, making the sensor array flexible rather than fixed by the hardware.

My Master’s thesis approached this flexibility as an experimental-design and inverse-problem question. I compared Leadfield- and Laplace-based spatial sampling for on-scalp arrays ranging from 20 to 400 sensors.

Synthetic measurements were generated from 513 cortical source locations in the left hemisphere and reconstructed using both Minimum Norm Estimation and the Bayesian SESAME method. This allowed the effect of sensor geometry to be examined across two different inverse approaches.

Localization improves as sensor density increases before approaching a noise-dependent saturation regime. The interactive viewer connects the original sensor layouts with cortical sensitivity and source-localization metrics in the same 3D geometry.

Different measurements.
Same mathematical habit.

Across optical imaging, photon-counting systems and electromagnetic measurements, I am interested in the same underlying structure: identify the quantity that matters, understand how the instrument encodes it, formulate the resulting inference problem, and exploit that structure computationally.
